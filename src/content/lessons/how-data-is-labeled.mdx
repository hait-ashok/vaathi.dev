Behind many models is a quieter job: people (and sometimes tools) **label** examples so a computer can learn or be checked. A label is just a clear answer attached to an input.

## What a label looks like

| Input | Example label |
| --- | --- |
| An email | `spam` / `not spam` |
| A support ticket | Topic: `billing` |
| A paragraph | Summary written by a human |
| A chatbot reply | `helpful` / `harmful` / `needs fix` |

Labels can be categories, scores, corrected text, or “preferred answer A over B.” The point is the same: **teach or measure** against something humans agreed is useful.

## Why teams bother

1. **Supervised learning** — Train a model to predict the label from new inputs (classic “show many examples”).
2. **Evaluation** — Score a model against labeled test sets (including metrics like [BLEU / ROUGE](/learn/foundations/bleu-and-rouge) when references exist).
3. **Fine-tuning / preference data** — Steer a general model toward a style, domain, or safer behavior using curated examples.

This sits inside the [how a model is built](/learn/foundations/how-a-model-is-built) pipeline: data quality often matters more than clever code.

## Humans, guidelines, and agreement

- **Guidelines** — Written rules (“count sarcasm as spam,” “don’t invent medical advice”). Vague guidelines → messy labels.
- **Human labeling** — People apply the guidelines; hard cases get discussion, not vibes.
- **Agreement** — If two labelers disagree often, the task or guidelines need work. Perfect agreement isn’t required; *consistent enough* is the goal.

See also: [Glossary — Label](/glossary#label), [Supervised learning](/glossary#supervised-learning).

## What you don’t need yet

Labeling platforms, workforce vendors, or MLOps dashboards. As a beginner, notice when a product claim rests on **labeled examples** versus raw internet text — and treat labels as human judgments, not absolute truth.

## Takeaway

**Labels turn opinions into training and test signals.** Clear guidelines beat heroic volume.
